    About VoiceIt

    VoiceIt is a rapid prototyping tool for products that listen and speak, such as smart speakers. People's experience of such a product depends on its timing, wording and voice, and on who else is present. It might give useful advice but interrupt at the wrong moment, speak too formally, or address the wrong person. That is hard to judge on paper. In VoiceIt, you write the behaviour as a script and play it back with synthetic voices, screen content, light and sound.

    A prototype combines a form and a behaviour. The form is the physical shape of the product: one image. The behaviour is a script of how the product acts: what it says and when, what it shows on its screen, and what its light and sounds do. Any behaviour can be played with any form, to test whether the two suit each other and to compare alternatives.

    The character is the impression people form of who the product is when they play a behaviour with a form, and it is what you judge. A script gives the character a name (character: Night light); its lines, timing, screen content, light and sound define how the product behaves.

    Workflow

    1. Behaviour. Tell your agent, in your own words, who the product is and how it should behave. It writes the script into design/behaviours/. Play it, and tell your agent what to change: less, later, softer, not in front of her.
    2. Form. Sketch the product on paper, have it rendered, mark up the render and render again (system/FORM.md). Save the image in design/forms/.
    3. Judge. Play a behaviour with different forms, and play the behaviours one after another. The rhythm strips in the list show them side by side.
    4. Watch. Press Watch to let form, screen and light fill the window, as an audience would see them. Space plays and pauses, the arrow keys step through the lines, Escape brings you back to where you were.

    Judging a design

    • Fit. Does this behaviour suit this form? Try it in another one.
    • Coherence. Is it the same character from one script to the next, for example at night and during visiting hour?
    • The details that make a character. When does it speak first, and when does it wait? How much does it say? Whom does it address when several people are there? What does it keep to itself, or show instead of saying? How does it handle doubt and mistakes? Is it a servant, a companion, a guardian, a colleague?

    Voices and timing

    Voices come from your browser and only approximate the voice named in the script. Durations (≈) are estimates; real browser speech may take a little longer.

    Background

    VoiceIt stages behaviour the way a screenplay stages a scene: written first, then played. It is related to experience prototyping and enactment, where designers act out a product's behaviour to judge it. It prototypes a product's role and its look and feel over time; designers use it to make and compare many rough variations early on.

    A product's character, in design research, is a coherent quality that holds across its appearance and its behaviour. Among several people, designers also have to decide whom the product addresses, who merely overhears, and when the product should leave people alone.

    The sources behind these ideas, and further reading, are in the Literature tab.

    Voices, gender and stereotypes

    Why do assistants sound and behave the way they do? These readings examine how designers construct gender through voice, manner and interaction.

    • UN News (2019). Are robots sexist? UN report shows gender bias in talking digital tech. A short summary of UNESCO's report I'd blush if I could: female default voices and submissive replies built into assistants, and what the report recommends.
    • Sutton, S. J. (2020). Gender ambiguous, not genderless: Designing gender in voice user interfaces with sensitivity. CUI '20. Listeners assign gender to a voice, and more than pitch does it; a "genderless" voice is undone by everything else in the design.
    • Strengers, Y. & Kennedy, J. (2020). The Smart Wife. MIT Press. How smart-home assistants are modelled on the 1950s housewife, and why that needs a redesign.
    • Nass, C. & Brave, S. (2005). Wired for Speech. MIT Press. A decade of experiments: people respond to machine voices socially, including with gender stereotypes and personality judgements.

    Prototyping behaviour

    What kind of prototype a VoiceIt performance is, and where the method comes from.

    • Houde, S. & Hill, C. (1997). What do prototypes prototype? In Handbook of Human-Computer Interaction. Prototypes explore role, look and feel, or implementation; VoiceIt deliberately explores the first two over time.
    • Buchenau, M. & Fulton Suri, J. (2000). Experience prototyping. DIS 2000. Understanding a design by experiencing it, not by looking at it: the idea behind playing a behaviour.
    • Buxton, B. (2007). Sketching User Experiences. Morgan Kaufmann. Many quick, cheap variations to argue about, before one refined prototype.
    • Völkel, S. T., Meindl, S. & Hussmann, H. (2021). Manipulating and evaluating levels of personality perceptions of voice assistants through enactment-based dialogue design. CUI '21. Actors wrote and performed dialogues for assistant personalities, which 156 people then rated: the closest research method to VoiceIt.
    • Dertien, E., van Delden, R. & Reidsma, D. (2024). Improvisation theatre as HRI simulation tool. MOCO '24. Improvised theatre to design a robot's behaviour, used in a Dutch MSc course and with care professionals: the live counterpart to scripted performances.

    The character of things

    Form and behaviour together make a character; people read it at a glance.

    • Janlert, L.-E. & Stolterman, E. (1997). The character of things. Design Studies 18(3). A thing's character is a coherent set of qualities across appearance and behaviour alike: the idea behind judging fit and coherence.
    • Govers, P. C. M., Hekkert, P. & Schoormans, J. P. L. (2002). Happy, cute and tough: Can designers create a product personality that consumers understand? From Delft: people read a product's personality from its appearance.
    • Mugge, R., Govers, P. C. M. & Schoormans, J. P. L. (2009). The development and testing of a product personality scale. Design Studies 30(3). A way to put words to the personality people see in a form.

    Among several people

    A product that listens is rarely alone with one user. It has to decide whom it addresses, who merely overhears, and when to leave people alone.

    • Goffman, E. (1981). Footing. In Forms of Talk (first published in Semiotica, 1979). Addressed and unaddressed hearers, bystanders and overhearers: the vocabulary for a product in a shared room.
    • Porcheron, M., Fischer, J. E., Reeves, S. & Sharples, S. (2018). Voice interfaces in everyday life. CHI 2018. Recordings of Amazon Echo in family homes: talk to the device is woven into talk among the people around it.
    • Luria, M. et al. (2020). Social boundaries for personal agents in the interpersonal space of the home. CHI 2020. Families reacting to storyboards of agent behaviour: roles, ownership, privacy and when an agent should stay out.

    When to speak up

    Initiative is one of the strongest signs of character: speaking first, waiting, or staying quiet.

    • Ju, W. (2015). The Design of Implicit Interactions. Interactions sorted by who takes the initiative and how much attention they ask: a grid for voice, light and silence.
    • Zargham, N. et al. (2022). Understanding circumstances for desirable proactive behaviour of voice assistants: The proactivity dilemma. CUI '22. People welcome proactivity when it matters, and fear it when others are talking.
    • Bérubé, C. et al. (2024). Proactive behavior in voice assistants: A systematic review and conceptual model. Context, initiation and action: a checklist for how a product steps in, from a signal to a spoken suggestion.
    • Oh, J. et al. (2024). Better to ask than assume: Proactive voice assistants' communication strategies that respect user agency in a smart home environment. CHI 2024. How a proactive assistant can leave control with the person.

    Mistakes and accountability

    What a product does when it gets something wrong often says more about its character than a smooth answer.

    • Alizadeh, F. et al. (2024). Voice assistants' accountability through explanatory dialogues. CUI '24. Nineteen households over nineteen months: explanations that unfold in conversation, not as one-off answers.
    • Reineke, S. & Helmer, H. (2024). User practices in dealing with trouble in interactions with virtual assistants in German. Discourse & Communication. The work people do to repair failed exchanges: repeating, altering, insisting.

    From Delft

    • Nicenboim, I., Giaccardi, E. & Redström, J. (2023). Designing more-than-human AI: Experiments on situated conversations and silences. diid 80. Prototypes of agents that listen differently, including deliberate silence.
    • Nicenboim, I. (2024). Designing-with AI: More-than-human design in/through practice. PhD thesis, TU Delft. Whose voices conversational agents are built to hear, and design tactics to change that.

    Getting started

    You need the Claude desktop app (its Code tab) or the Codex app, signed in; Git (check with git --version in a terminal; on a Mac the first git command offers to install it, on Windows install Git for Windows); and Node.js 18 or newer (nodejs.org, check with node --version).

    1. Make a folder for your course work, for example Documents/IDEM307.
    2. Get VoiceIt into it. Open your agent with a new session on that folder (Claude: Code → new session → choose the folder; Codex: open the folder) and type:
      Clone https://github.com/kortuem/voiceit.git into this folder.
      A folder voiceit appears. (Already cloned it in a terminal, as in the README? Skip this step and open that folder in step 3.)
    3. Start a new session on the voiceit folder itself. The folder carries its own instructions for the agent, in AGENTS.md; Claude Code and Codex read them by themselves when they work in this folder.
    4. To be sure, start every new session with:
      Read AGENTS.md and follow it.
      The agent replies that it has read the instructions.
    5. Start VoiceIt. Open a terminal in the voiceit folder and type node system/bin/preview. Your browser opens VoiceIt. Leave the terminal open while you work; Ctrl+C stops it. (No Node? See Troubleshooting: VoiceIt also runs online.)
      Opening a terminal in the voiceit folder
      • Mac: open Terminal, type cd (with a space), drag the voiceit folder onto the window, press Return.
      • Windows: open the voiceit folder in File Explorer, click the address bar, type cmd, press Enter.
    6. Check the sound. Open VoiceIt's Setup tab and press Play the voices. You hear each voice of the catalogue as your laptop plays it (fewer different ones if your laptop has only a few), and the checks under Needed to play say Yes. If a voice sounds wrong for its description, a man's voice for a high voice for example, choose another laptop voice for it in the table below the button.
    7. Better voices (optional, takes a few minutes). The standard voices sound robotic; better ones make the scripts much easier to judge. VoiceIt picks the best voices it finds by itself.
      • Mac: System Settings › Accessibility › Spoken Content › System voice › Manage Voices…. Under English, download a few voices marked Premium or Enhanced, for example Jamie, Daniel, Serena, Karen and Ava (low and high voices both help). Restart the browser, then play the voices again.
      • Windows: use Microsoft Edge, which offers natural-sounding online voices (their names end in "Natural"). More voices can be added under Settings › Time & language › Speech › Manage voices; not every added voice is available to the browser. (Not yet tested on Windows.)

    Next: the tutorial continues with step 2, Play an example, and has the phrases for your agent.
